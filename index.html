Dendrites — where the neuron receives most of its connections. One cell can receive contacts from up to 10,000 others.

Soma (cell body) — holds the nucleus and the organelles that keep the cell alive and stocked with transmitter.

Axon — the transmission cable. Information travels from the cell body toward the terminals; in some sensory and motor cells the axon is about a meter long.

Synapse — the terminal. The presynaptic ending faces the next cell across a narrow synaptic cleft, and talks to it chemically, not by touching it.

Plate I

The neuron

The brain’s phenomenal features would not be possible without the billions of neurons that make it up — and, importantly, the connections between them. Unlike most cells, neurons are polarized: they have distinct regions with distinct jobs. Dendrites listen, the soma houses the machinery, the axon carries the message, and the synapse delivers it.

The presynaptic cell is never directly connected to the postsynaptic one. The two are separated by a gap, the synaptic cleft. So to communicate, the presynaptic terminal releases a chemical messenger from small vesicles (exocytosis), the transmitter diffuses across the gap, and it binds receptors on the far side. Receptor binding opens ion channels, changing the electrical potential of the postsynaptic cell.

Tap the painted pins to read each region. And notice the hidden point of this plate: because signaling among neurons is electrical, to understand circuits we first have to understand the electrical properties of a single cell.

Deeper: pre- and post- are roles, not cell types

The tan neuron that is presynaptic to the aqua one is itself postsynaptic to three other neurons. Most “presynaptic” neurons are “postsynaptic” to some other neuron. Each cell can contact up to 10,000 postsynaptic cells, so the potential complexity of the network is vast.

Plate II

The resting potential

Take a fine glass microelectrode — a capillary tube stretched under heat to a tip about one micron across — fill it with conducting solution, and connect it to a recorder. Outside the cell, in the extracellular medium, it reads zero. Everything out there is at the same potential.

Push the tip through the membrane and the pen snaps down: the inside of the cell is about −60 millivolts relative to the outside. That is the resting potential, and in the absence of stimulation it is constant for indefinite periods. Every cell in the body has a resting potential; what makes neurons (and muscle) special is that theirs is changeable.

Method: what a microelectrode actually is

A small piece of glass capillary tubing, stretched under heat to a tip on the order of 1 micron, filled with a conducting solution and connected to a recording device such as an oscilloscope or chart recorder.

Plate III

Threshold and the spike

Now impale the neuron with a second, stimulating electrode wired through a switch to a battery. Orient the positive pole in, close the switch, and the inside of the cell becomes somewhat more positive — a depolarization. Small batteries give small deflections. But wind the battery up, and at a certain voltage something extraordinary happens.

Two features of the action potential are worth carrying forward. First, it is all-or-nothing: at or above threshold you get a full spike, and a bigger stimulus gives the same spike — like a match lighting a fuse; a hotter match doesn’t burn the fuse brighter. Second, it is brief — a few milliseconds. And once initiated at one region of the cell, it propagates along the axon without shrinking, all the way to the synapse, like a burning fuse.

Deeper: why this is called frequency modulation

Engineers call this way of carrying information pulse frequency modulation. Harder touch, stronger stretch, brighter light — each arrives in the central nervous system as a higher rate of identical action potentials, and in the motor system more spikes in a motor neuron mean stronger muscle contraction.

Plate IV

Excitement meets inhibition

An action potential in the green neuron releases transmitter that depolarizes the blue postsynaptic cell — an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP), a “go” signal. One spike is generally not enough; but two EPSPs arriving close together in time, or on neighboring dendrites, can summate to threshold. The red neuron releases a transmitter with the opposite effect: a hyperpolarizing IPSP, a “stop” signal that pulls the membrane away from threshold.

The postsynaptic neuron is an adding machine. It sums excitation and inhibition across time (temporal summation) and across the surface of its dendrites (spatial summation). At or above threshold: spike. Below: silence. Try letting the red neuron fire just before the green ones, and watch the same excitatory volley get gated off.

Model & assumptions

This demo uses a simplified descriptive model, not a biophysical simulation: EPSPs peak at +11 mV from rest with exponential decay (τ ≈ 8 ms) and presynaptic spikes 6 ms apart, the IPSP dips −14 mV with slower decay (τ ≈ 15 ms), and threshold sits 10 mV above rest. Reference cases validated against the chapter: one EPSP alone stays subthreshold (peak −52 mV); two EPSPs summate past threshold (−49.8 mV) and fire; the same two plus a preceding IPSP peak below threshold (−54 mV); three EPSPs fire. Real summation is sublinear and location-dependent, so treat the traces as an illustrative reconstruction, not a measurement.

Plate V

A field guide to circuit motifs

A neuron can receive contacts from up to 10,000 presynaptic cells and can contact up to 10,000 postsynaptic cells. The possible circuit topologies are enormous — yet much of what a nervous system does is built from a small set of recurring microcircuit motifs:

Simple as these look, such microcircuits mediate reflexes, process sensory information, generate locomotion, and mediate learning and memory. Larger functions — object recognition, cognition — come from macrocircuits built out of many embedded microcircuits.

And the same shapes recur within single cells, as biochemical “nanocircuits.”

Deeper: nanocircuits — the same loops inside one cell

Endogenous bursting. Some neurons fire rhythmic bursts of spikes with no synaptic input at all. A calcium-permeable channel (gSI) depolarizes the cell until it fires; the firing lets Ca²⁺ in; accumulated Ca²⁺ inhibits the very channel that let it in; buffers and pumps (INaCa, ICaP) slowly bring Ca²⁺ back down; disinhibited, the channel opens again. A parabolic burster repeats this every ten to fifteen seconds — an excitatory-inhibitory feedback loop made of molecules, not synapses.

Circadian rhythms. The per gene makes per mRNA, which makes PER protein, which returns to the nucleus and represses its own gene. PER degrades over roughly 24 hours; as it disappears, repression lifts and the cycle repeats. Neurons in the suprachiasmatic nucleus run this loop to time melatonin, cortisol, and body temperature.

In both cases: excitation, feedback inhibition, waning of the inhibitor, and a new cycle. The motif is conserved from molecules to circuits.

Plate VI

The knee-jerk, stroke by stroke

A neurologist taps the knee with a rubber hammer. The muscle stretches; stretch-sensitive sensory neurons fire; the impulse travels into the spinal cord, where the sensory axon splits into two branches — and a full behavior comes out the other end.

Why clinicians use it

The knee-jerk (stretch reflex) is a simple way to examine the integrity of sensory and motor pathways in the spinal cord: sensory neurons whose axons split in the cord, an excitatory branch, a disynaptic inhibitory branch, and the motor output to muscle.

Plate VII

Edges, and the illusion of bands

Stare at a border between a dark gray band and a light gray one. Each band is uniform, yet the light side looks lighter near the edge, and the dark side darker. Edge enhancement is the retina sharpening the world — and lateral inhibition explains it.

The same circuit also manufactures an illusion. Lay one thin horizontal bar across a painted gradient of light and dark vertical bands and the bar seems uneven — darker where it crosses light bands, lighter where it crosses dark ones. Mask the background and the bar is revealed to be perfectly uniform. The bar was never the problem; your retina was.

Plate VIII

Synthesis: how to look at the paintings

Step back and look at the whole series again, and you can now see one mechanism compounded into a mind:

  1. A neuron is polarized — −60 millivolts of stored, waiting difference.
  2. Push that difference 10 mV toward zero and it snaps into an all-or-nothing spike, +40 mV, half a millisecond wide.
  3. Intensity isn’t carried by spike size, but by spike frequency.
  4. At the synapse, spikes become chemical votes — EPSPs say go, IPSPs say stop — and the postsynaptic cell is an adding machine that fires or stays silent.
  5. Wire excitation and inhibition into small motifs — relay, gate, fan-out, fan-in, contrast, latch, damper — and you get reflexes, edges, rhythms, and memory; the same feedback shapes recur as nanocircuits inside single cells.

The wonderful device is not that the brain has 100 billion neurons — it is that they communicate in a highly structured manner, forming networks. Every painting in this atlas was the same painting at a different scale.
